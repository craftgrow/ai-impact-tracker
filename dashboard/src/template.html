What the importer found

Sessions measured
__AUDIT_SESSIONS__
Local Claude Code and Cowork transcripts, read straight from disk. Token counts come from each assistant turn’s own usage block, so these are measurements rather than estimates.
Measured, not estimated
__AUDIT_EXACT_PCT__%
Share of the energy on this page that comes from exact token counts. The rest is estimated — self-reported entries and any data export — and is marked as such in the table below.
Cache share
__AUDIT_CACHE_PCT__%
Of __AUDIT_TOKENS__ tokens processed, __AUDIT_CACHE_TOKENS__ were cache reads — context re-sent on every turn. Self-reporting is structurally blind to this, which is why it undercounts so badly.

There are two ways to know what a session cost. Claude can estimate its own token use at the end of a turn — which only fires when it remembers, needs a rule in every project’s CLAUDE.md, and cannot see cached context at all. Or you can read the transcripts the desktop app and Claude Code already write to disk, where every turn records its exact usage. tools/import_transcripts.py does the second: it de-duplicates on a hash of each file’s path, and supersedes a self-reported entry only when a transcript session for the same project covers it. It is idempotent — re-running picks up only what is new.

Retention limits how far back this can see. The importer reads transcripts on disk, and those are pruned over time. The earliest session in this log is __AUDIT_FIRST__ — that is not a start date, only where the surviving files begin. __AUDIT_PRUNED__ already reference files that have since been deleted; those entries stay in the log, but nothing further can be recovered from before the window. Usage from before you started measuring will always look lighter than it was.

Two known limitations. Every Cowork session lands in a single bucket, because those transcript files carry no project field — per-project attribution would need a classifier that does not exist yet. And Claude Code projects are named after the raw working-directory folder rather than any tidy name, across __AUDIT_PROJECTS__ distinct names in this log.

What this does not claim

Energy per token is modelled, not metered. Nobody outside a data-centre operator can measure the draw of a single request, so this uses published per-model figures and a grid-intensity assumption you can edit in the methodology panel below. Cache reads are costed at a fraction of the input rate, and that fraction is an assumption governing most of the tokens here. The comparisons are sourced and the arithmetic is shown, but the inputs carry real uncertainty. Treat this as a well-founded estimate of scale — good enough to tell a rounding error from a real problem, not good enough to audit anyone.

Sources & caveats
Per-token model energy rates — from the environmental-impact-tracker methodology (Anthropic Cowork skill): Opus 50/250, Sonnet 30/150, Haiku 10/50 Wh per million input/output tokens.
Water usage — Li et al., “Making AI Less Thirsty: Uncovering and Addressing the Secret Water Footprint of AI Models” (2023), arXiv:2304.03271. Data-center water is highly location- and cooling-dependent.
Electricity & data-centre energy — International Energy Agency (IEA), Electricity 2024 and data-centre analyses, iea.org.
CO₂e equivalencies (miles driven, etc.) — US EPA Greenhouse Gas Equivalencies Calculator, epa.gov. Passenger vehicle ≈ 404 g CO₂/mile.
Grid carbon intensity — global average ≈ 0.40 kg CO₂e/kWh (IEA); varies widely by region and by how much renewable energy the data-center uses. Edit the value above to match your grid.
Usage grade (A+–F) — a 0–100 optimization score, not an absolute verdict: model efficiency (50 pts, from effective Wh per million tokens — lighter models like Haiku/Sonnet score higher than Opus), session leanness (30 pts, from average Wh per session), and mitigation commitment (20 pts, from the checklist above). It reflects how you use AI relative to the most-efficient choices, and updates with your filters and checkboxes.
Comparison factors — phone charge ≈ 12 Wh; LED bulb 10 W; kettle boil ≈ 100 Wh; water bottle 500 mL; glass 240 mL; running tap ≈ 100 mL/s; car ≈ 404 g CO₂/mile (EPA) ≈ 251 g/km; a mature tree absorbs ≈ 57 g CO₂/day.
Efficiency (Wh per million tokens) — each session's logged energy divided by its logged tokens. It is the only efficiency figure this log shape supports; the input/output split cannot be recovered from total energy alone, because entries costed with a blended rate back-solve to impossible values. New entries record input_tokens and output_tokens so the real split becomes measurable going forward.
Home energy — US household averages of 881 kWh/month of electricity and 4,717 ft³/month of natural gas, at 0.12 lb CO₂ per ft³ (US EPA, Household Carbon Footprint Calculator). Grid intensity defaults to 367 g CO₂e/kWh, the US average generation rate (US EIA, 2023). Both are editable above.
Driving — a typical US passenger vehicle emits ≈ 400 g CO₂ per mile and ≈ 4.6 t CO₂/year at 11,500 miles and 22.2 mpg (US EPA, Greenhouse Gas Emissions from a Typical Passenger Vehicle). Petrol figures are scaled by your mpg; EV figures use ≈ 0.35 kWh/mile against your grid intensity.
Vehicle manufacturing (embodied) — building a new petrol sedan releases ≈ 6 t CO₂; a comparable EV ≈ 10 t, mostly from the battery. Keeping a car therefore defers those emissions rather than removing them, and a much more efficient replacement can pay its manufacturing back over time.
Flights — one transatlantic round trip ≈ 1.6 t CO₂e (Wynes & Nicholas 2017, Environmental Research Letters 12:074024). The short-haul figure is scaled by distance from that number and is rougher.
Diet — kg CO₂e/day standardised to 2,000 kcal (Scarborough et al. 2023, Nature Food): high-meat 10.24 and vegan 2.47 are reported directly; low-meat 5.86 uses the paper's 57.2% ratio; medium-meat and vegetarian are interpolated between them and are the softest numbers here.
Children and household size — treated only as household composition: more people share the home's energy, which lowers each person's share of it. The widely-cited figure of 58.6 t CO₂e/year for "having one fewer child" (Wynes & Nicholas 2017) is deliberately not used anywhere in this dashboard. It assigns a parent half of a child's projected lifetime emissions plus a quarter of the grandchildren's and so on, using historical emission rates that assume no decarbonisation — an accounting choice that is heavily contested and that this tool takes no position on.
Avoided emissions are not offsets — the ledger is shown beside your AI footprint and never subtracted from it. Energy consumed cannot be un-consumed; reductions elsewhere are separate real-world wins. Every line is counterfactual — measured against a baseline you set yourself — so it is only as honest as that baseline.
These figures are order-of-magnitude estimates. Real impact depends on model, hardware, data-center location, cooling, grid mix, and caching. Treat trends as more reliable than absolute values.
